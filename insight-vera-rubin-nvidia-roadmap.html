The NVIDIA roadmap after Blackwell: what Vera Rubin means for buyers.

Vera Rubin is not a GPU upgrade. It is a procurement decision — and for most enterprise buyers, the window to make it well opened months ago. Here is what the architecture means, why the timeline is not what it looks like, and what the decisions in front of you actually are.

Published October 22, 2026

The conversations about Vera Rubin that matter are not happening in public. They are happening between NVIDIA's top-tier channel and a small set of buyers who got into those relationships before the platform had a commercial name. By the time a platform is announced and detailed enough to brief a procurement team, the allocation decision for the first production wave has already been made.

That is not unique to Vera Rubin. It is how every NVIDIA generation has worked at the front of the cycle. What changes is the scale of what is at stake — and therefore the cost of being a late entrant.

What Vera Rubin actually is

Vera Rubin is a two-component platform name. "Rubin" is the GPU architecture — the NVR100, which succeeds the Blackwell GPU. "Vera" is the CPU — an ARM-based processor that succeeds the Grace CPU used in the current Blackwell platform, the GB300 NVL72.

The flagship system form factor is the Vera Rubin NVL72: 72 Rubin GPUs in a single NVLink 6 domain, liquid-cooled, per rack. This is the direct architectural successor to the GB300 NVL72 — same form factor, same liquid-cooled infrastructure class, same single-NVLink-domain-per-rack topology. What changes is everything underneath: HBM4 in place of HBM3e, NVLink 6 in place of NVLink 5, and NVIDIA's target of approximately 2× the AI training throughput of Blackwell Ultra at the same rack footprint.

The HBM4 step deserves attention. The jump from HBM2e (H100) to HBM3 (H100 SXM5) to HBM3e (B200) to HBM4 (Vera Rubin) follows a trajectory of roughly doubling memory bandwidth and materially increasing capacity per generation. Memory has been the binding constraint on large-model inference — context length, model size, batch efficiency — across every generation since Hopper. Vera Rubin's HBM4 continues that trajectory.

NVLink 6 raises the GPU interconnect bandwidth within the NVL72 domain above NVLink 5. For training workloads that are already NVLink-bandwidth-bound at Blackwell scales, this matters. For inference workloads, it matters for the model sizes that will exist when Vera Rubin is in production.

This is not a minor stepping-stone platform. It is a full generation, designed for the workloads that will exist in 2027 and 2028 — not the workloads of 2025.

The procurement timeline problem

Here is the structural problem with buying compute infrastructure on a published roadmap: by the time the information is detailed enough to make a procurement decision, the decision has already been made for you.

At the front of the cycle — six to twelve months before commercial availability — there is very little public specification. But that is when allocation is being reserved. The buyers who are in those conversations are not there because they waited for the data sheet. They are there because they have standing with the channel, and the channel let them in early.

When detailed specifications arrive, two things have already happened: the first production allocation has largely been committed, and the buyers who got early positions have already set the terms that late entrants will have to beat or accept.

The lead time on a platform relationship is longer than the lead time on the hardware. The window is not "before commercial availability." It is "before the people inside the allocation process stopped taking new introductions."

For Vera Rubin, that window is open now — but it is not open indefinitely. Buyers who are not already in standing with the right channel partners need an introduction. That introduction is time-sensitive in a way that hardware lead times are not.

Facility implications

The facility case for Vera Rubin is simpler than it looks, because NVIDIA has maintained consistent infrastructure across the rack-scale NVL form factor.

GB300 NVL72 is already the highest-density, highest-power platform in the current generation: liquid-cooled at rack scale, high power per rack, requiring facility infrastructure that most enterprise data centers were not designed for. Vera Rubin NVL72 is in the same cooling class. A facility designed for GB300 NVL72 is a facility that Vera Rubin can run in — the transition does not require a new build.

The practical consequence is important: the infrastructure decision and the Vera Rubin procurement decision are the same decision, made at the same time. A buyer who is designing a facility for GB300 NVL72 should be having the Vera Rubin conversation in parallel — not as a future upgrade, but as the platform the facility is sized for from the start.

Power density warrants attention. At approximately 2× the training performance of GB300 in the same footprint, Vera Rubin may draw higher power per rack than GB300. The exact figure will be confirmed at commercial specification. Size the facility with headroom above the GB300 NVL72 figure to avoid a facility constraint that arrives with the hardware.

For buyers who are not yet designing for GB300 NVL72, the single facility design covers both. The liquid cooling plant, the power density, the rack infrastructure — they are the same conversation whether you are designing for GB300 or Vera Rubin. Starting that design now means being ready for the platform that delivers next year and the one that delivers the year after.

The B300 vs Vera Rubin decision

This question comes up early in almost every conversation about Vera Rubin, and the framing is usually wrong. Buyers ask it as a performance comparison — "is Vera Rubin worth waiting for?" — when the actual decision is a timeline and budget question.

Vera Rubin is clearly more capable. Approximately 2× the training performance of Blackwell Ultra, HBM4, NVLink 6 — these are not marginal improvements. The performance case for Vera Rubin over B300 is not close.

The question is when you need the compute, and what it costs to wait for it.

B300 (Blackwell Ultra) delivers sooner. Allocation is available now through the right channel relationships, lead times are well-understood, and the facility infrastructure is the same as Vera Rubin — liquid-cooled NVL rack-scale, the same build whether you are going to B300 or Vera Rubin. The cost basis for B300 is known.

Vera Rubin delivers more performance. Allocation at this stage is restricted. Lead times will extend before they shorten. The cost basis at commercial availability will be set by what early buyers paid, and early buyers are in those conversations now.

The decision framework:

  • Compute need is certain and near-term: B300, with Vera Rubin as the follow-on generation once the facility is designed and operating. The two are not mutually exclusive — a facility built for B300 NVL72 is a facility that upgrades to Vera Rubin NVL72.
  • Compute need is 2027+ and facility readiness is already planned: Vera Rubin as the initial platform, using the same facility that would have housed B300. No ramp through an intermediate generation required.
  • Multi-year capacity program: Both, in sequence. Reserve B300 for near-term delivery and get into the Vera Rubin allocation process while that window is still open.

What this is not: a binary choice where picking one closes the other. The facility is the same. The allocation relationships are the same. The only variable is timing.

What this means for GPUaaS buyers

GPUaaS and reserved-cluster buyers face a compressed version of the same problem.

The neocloud and GPUaaS operators who will offer Vera Rubin capacity are already having internal conversations about it. The ones that get early allocation will be able to offer reserved capacity at commercial launch. The ones that are late to those conversations will be offering it six to twelve months after everyone else — by which time the market will have priced accordingly.

For enterprise buyers who want GPUaaS on Vera Rubin rather than owned hardware, the relevant question is not "when can I book it?" It is "which GPUaaS operators are going to have it at launch?" That answer is being determined now, based on which operators got early allocation positions.

An advisory relationship that already knows which operators are in those conversations is materially different from approaching the open market at commercial availability and asking who has capacity. By that point, the capacity that matters has already been allocated.

The roadmap beyond Rubin

Rubin Ultra is the expected follow-on to Vera Rubin, with NVIDIA targeting a roughly two-year cadence from Vera Rubin to Rubin Ultra. The exact timeline is not public, and NVIDIA has compressed its roadmap once already in the current generation — treating the stated timeline as fixed is a planning error.

The implications for infrastructure planning are structural:

First, the technology transition cost is declining with each generation. Vera Rubin and Rubin Ultra will be in the same liquid-cooled rack-scale infrastructure class as GB300. A facility built today for GB300 NVL72 will be the same facility that runs Vera Rubin and — almost certainly — Rubin Ultra. The facility amortizes across multiple generations in a way that air-cooled facilities could not.

Second, the cadence is accelerating, not slowing. From Hopper (H100 launch, 2022) to Blackwell (B200 launch, 2024) was two years. From Blackwell to Vera Rubin appears to be a similar or compressed timeline. A five-year infrastructure strategy that locks compute for five years and calls it done is no longer a viable frame. The compute procurement cycle and the infrastructure design cycle need to be treated as continuous and overlapping, not sequential and one-time.

Third, early access compounds. The buyers who were in the Blackwell allocation at the front of the cycle got better terms, earlier delivery windows, and better relationships with the channel partners who will be the gatekeepers for Vera Rubin. That advantage does not reset at each new generation — it builds. The cost of being late to Blackwell is partly measured in the disadvantage it creates for accessing Vera Rubin.
