02

We can bring the whole deal, not just the demand

It is the first thing operators ask about, so it is worth answering directly.

A capacity deal often stalls because it needs three things that live in three different places: an offtaker with real demand, energised capacity that fits it, and compute to fill it. Normally those are three separate searches, run by parties who never meet, on three different timelines.

We hold relationships at every layer, which means we are not limited to bringing you a tenant. Depending on what the deal needs, we can bring the offtaker, source the compute that lands in your halls, and structure the term so the financing case works — assembling a transaction that would not otherwise have existed.

Every party involved knows we are working every part of it, and we say so at the outset. Disclosure is the mechanism that keeps this straight, not exclusivity, and it is reinforced by the fact that we hold no inventory and take no position in the outcome. We do not earn more from one configuration than another.

Within that, knowing how buyers think is exactly what makes the demand we bring worth taking:

  • It is priced to close. We know what comparable deals are actually clearing at, so a requirement arrives with a realistic expectation attached rather than one anchored on a number nobody in this market is paying.
  • The objections are already surfaced. Redundancy tier, ramp, power pass-through mechanics, exit rights — the terms that stall a deal in week six have usually been discussed by week one.
  • The requirement is real. Most of what a capacity sales team spends time on is not a deal. We absorb that filtering before it reaches you.
  • You are not bidding blind. Where we can say so without breaching a confidence, you will know whether you are the shortlist or the stalking horse.

A buyer who overpays becomes a difficult tenant. An operator who wins on a term the buyer later regrets does not get the renewal. Neither side is served by a deal built on a wrong number.

That is not a diplomatic position, it is a commercial one. We are only useful to this market for as long as every side keeps taking our calls, which makes a deal that holds up in year three worth more to us than a deal that closes this quarter.

03

The KYC and compliance work, done before it reaches you

This is the part operators tell us costs them the most time and creates the most risk, and it is where we do the heaviest lifting.

In GPU and capacity deals, the entity that signs is frequently not the entity whose workload consumes the compute. A neocloud contracts for a named offtaker; a reseller sits between you and the balance sheet that actually stands behind a five-year term. Working out who is really there is not a formality — it is the difference between a contracted megawatt and a contracted risk.

We run that work first, and we run it alongside your team rather than handing you a file and stepping back.

  • Entity and ownership verification. The signing entity, the parent, the guarantor, and the beneficial ownership behind them.
  • Ultimate offtaker identification. The party whose own workload consumes the capacity and whose balance sheet carries the term. Named explicitly, with written consent before anything is disclosed to you.
  • Sanctions, AML and adverse media screening. Run before an introduction, not after a term sheet is circulating.
  • Signatory authority. Confirmation that the person negotiating can actually bind the entity, which is a more common failure point than it should be.
  • Commitment readiness. Budget approved, signatory identified, NDA in place, deposit capability established. If those are not in evidence, it is not an introduction yet.
  • Alongside your process, not instead of it. Your credit and compliance teams have their own standard and their own regulator. We work to it, supply what they need in the form they need it, and stay on the file until they sign it off.

Your compliance team should be verifying what we found, not starting from a blank page.

Everything gathered in this process is held in confidence and never disclosed without the offtaker's written consent. That protection is what makes offtakers willing to answer the questions honestly in the first place — which is precisely why the answers are worth something to you.

04

What the market actually commits to

Term expectations differ sharply by product, and a mismatch here stalls more deals than price does.

GPUaaS & compute offtake3–5 years
Colocation & capacity5–10 years

Compute offtakers commit in three-to-five-year windows, because that is roughly the horizon over which a GPU generation and a workload plan both stay credible. Data center operators underwrite colocation on five to ten years, because that is what the financing case requires.

The gap between those two numbers is where a great many deals quietly die. An operator asking a compute offtaker for a ten-year term is asking them to underwrite two hardware generations they cannot forecast; an offtaker offering three years on a build that needs seven does not clear the investment committee. The structures that bridge it — staged terms, expansion rights held at a pre-agreed basis, guarantor support, renewal mechanics tied to refresh cycles — are what we spend the negotiation on.

What do you actually do on KYC and compliance?

+

Entity and ownership verification, identification of the ultimate offtaker and the balance sheet standing behind the term, parent and guarantor confirmation, sanctions, AML and adverse media screening, and confirmation that the person negotiating can bind the entity.

All of it before an introduction, and all of it worked through alongside your own credit and compliance teams rather than handed over as a file. Your regulator and your standard govern the outcome — we supply what they need in the form they need it and stay on the file until it is signed off.

We already have a sales team. What do you add?
