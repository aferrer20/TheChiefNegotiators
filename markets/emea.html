Market · Europe, Middle East, Africa

EMEA. The famous markets are the closed ones.

Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin are where everyone looks and where almost nothing new connects. The megawatts are being delivered in the Nordics, Iberia and the Gulf — each of which charges a different price in regulation, latency or certainty.

01

Where the capacity actually is

  • —
    The Nordics — Norway, Sweden, Finland

    The strongest combination of power price, power cleanliness and connectability in Europe. Cold ambient air reduces cooling energy for most of the year, hydro and nuclear provide firm supply, and district heating networks make recovered heat a contracted revenue line rather than a sustainability claim. Latency to Frankfurt and London is real but tolerable, and irrelevant for training.

  • —
    Iceland

    Effectively unlimited clean firm power, a climate that removes most of the cooling problem, and the longest latency in the region. Excellent for training and batch, unsuitable for anything user-facing in continental Europe.

  • —
    FLAP-D — Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris, Dublin

    Deep, mature, expensive and largely full. Dublin has been effectively closed to new large grid connections for years. Amsterdam operates under restrictive local policy. Frankfurt is constrained on land and power and carries efficiency and heat-reuse obligations that land in your contract. London is constrained in the west. Paris is comparatively the most workable of the five. Use these markets for inference and for existing inventory; do not plan a new training campus around them.

  • —
    Iberia — Spain and Portugal

    The fastest-improving story in Europe. Abundant solar and wind, substantial land, subsea cable landings that improve latency to the Americas and Africa, and regional authorities that want the investment. Aragón in particular has absorbed a large volume of new commitments.

  • —
    Italy and southern Europe

    Milan has become a credible tier-one alternative with genuine connectivity and a substantial finance and enterprise base. Power cost is higher than Iberia and the permitting is slower, but the demand-side case is real.

  • —
    The Gulf — UAE and Saudi Arabia

    Power, land and capital are all available at a scale and speed Europe cannot match, and very large blocks can be committed quickly. The gating item is not physical: it is the export control and end-use regime governing the silicon, and that must be settled with certainty before commercial terms are worth discussing.

02

The regulatory layer, priced as a commercial term

EMEA is the region where compliance is most likely to change the deal rather than merely document it. These are the items that move numbers.

  1. Grid access as the primary gate. In several of the largest markets the question is not what a connection costs but whether one is available at all within your horizon. A site marketed as “powered” needs supporting documentation from the network operator, not a statement from the developer.
  2. Efficiency and heat reuse obligations. Several jurisdictions now impose facility-level efficiency targets and, in some cases, heat recovery duties. These arrive in your agreement as PUE commitments, reporting obligations and occasionally an obligation to make heat available. They are negotiable, but only if you identify them as commercial rather than administrative.
  3. Data residency and sectoral rules. Broad rules apply to personal data, and much stricter sectoral regimes apply in finance, health and the public sector. Training on non-personal corpora is far less constrained than most buyers assume, which is precisely what opens up the Nordics and Iberia.
  4. Export control on the silicon. Applies to where the hardware physically sits, who controls it and who can access it downstream. This is the first question in the Gulf and a live question anywhere with complex ownership.
  5. Energy contracting and price risk. European power pricing is more volatile and more politically exposed than North American pricing. Whether you take a fixed rate, a pass-through or a PPA is one of the largest economic decisions in the agreement, and it is frequently made by default.

Split deployments — training where power is cheap, inference where the users are — are common here and change which contracts must be tied together. The note on where capacity is actually available covers the current state, and capacity sourcing runs the comparison.

Questions

Asked in this market.

01

Is Dublin still closed to new data centers?

+

For practical purposes, new large grid connections in the greater Dublin area have been effectively unavailable for several years, and the workarounds involve bringing your own generation. Ireland remains excellent for existing inventory and for anything already connected; it is a poor choice for a new multi-megawatt requirement on a near-term date.

02

Why are the Nordics recommended so consistently?

+

Low-cost firm hydro, a cold climate that materially reduces cooling energy, an established heat reuse market that turns waste heat into a revenue line, and grid operators that have been broadly willing to connect large load. The cost is latency to the major European population centres, which matters for inference and not for training.

03

What does the German efficiency regime mean for a buyer?

+

German sites carry efficiency and heat-reuse obligations that fall on the facility, and they show up in your contract as PUE commitments, reporting duties and sometimes heat offtake arrangements. They are manageable, but they must be read as commercial terms rather than as an environmental annexe.

04

Is the Gulf a serious option for AI compute?

+

On power, land and capital, yes — it is one of the few places where very large blocks can be delivered quickly. The binding constraint is not physical, it is the export control and end-use regime around the silicon, and that has to be established with certainty before anything else is negotiated.

05

How much does GDPR actually constrain site selection?

+

Less than most buyers assume for model training on non-personal data, and considerably more for inference on customer data, regulated sectors and anything involving special category data. The distinction is worth making precisely, because it decides whether the Nordics and Iberia are on your list.

06

Can a European deployment be split across markets?

+

It is often the right answer — training in the Nordics or Iberia where power is cheap and abundant, inference in Frankfurt, London, Paris or Amsterdam where the users are. It complicates the contracts and usually improves the economics substantially.
